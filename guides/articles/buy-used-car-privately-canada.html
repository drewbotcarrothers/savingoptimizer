How to buy a used car privately in Canada without getting burned: a province-aware checklist

UVIP, liens, and a PPI — not a U.S. Craigslist template · About 16 minutes · By Andrew · Updated 15 Oct 2026

To buy a used car privately in Canada, prove the seller owns it (the VIN and photo ID match the ownership), prove nobody else has a claim on it (a lien search, such as Ontario’s $20 UVIP or a CARFAX Canada report with a lien check), then pay a shop you chose to inspect it before any money moves. Pay by bank draft or in-branch transfer after the paperwork, then register on your province’s timeline: in Ontario that is within 6 days, with 13% retail sales tax on the purchase price or the wholesale value, whichever is greater.

Private-sale ads look cheap because they skip dealer overhead — and sometimes because they skip a lien discharge, a branded title, or a rusted subframe. Canadian buyers who copy U.S. checklist blogs miss Ontario’s UVIP, provincial tax-on-transfer rules, and the fact that a CARFAX Canada report is not a mechanical inspection.

Whether used beats new at all is TCO. Insurance on the VIN is a quote, not a guess. Used EVs do not get EVAP.

Key takeaways

  • Match VIN on the dash/door, ownership permit, and listing. Meet the person named on the permit with photo ID. A “cousin selling for them” is a pause, not a story.
  • Ontario: seller must provide a UVIP ($20) — registration history, branding, odometer, liens. Buyers can also order one. Ontario.ca, updated 1 Oct 2026.
  • CARFAX Canada is extra history (accidents, U.S., service), not a UVIP clone. Confirm the product includes a lien check if you buy it ($77.95 with lien check vs $58.95 history-only, carfax.ca, checked 15 Oct 2026).
  • Independent PPI is non-negotiable on anything that is not a token amount. CAA-Quebec puts the average at $150–$250.
  • Pay after paperwork, not before. Bank draft or in-branch. Then tax, safety (where required), insurance, plates — in the order your province publishes. Ontario: register within 6 days and pay 13% RST on the price or wholesale value, whichever is greater.

How do you check the VIN, seller ID and ownership?

Match the VIN on the car to the ownership permit and the listing, and meet the person named on the permit with government photo ID. Before you argue about price, prove the object and the human.

  • Read the VIN on the windshield and the door sticker. Compare to the listing, the ownership (Ontario’s green permit; other provinces have their own cards), and any history report.
  • Ask for government photo ID that matches the name on the permit. If two names are on title, both should sign the transfer.
  • Look up the plate. A car “just imported” or “plates expired, sitting at my buddy’s” needs extra registry checks, not less.
  • Meet in daylight at the seller’s address or a police-station / ServiceOntario parking lot — not a random plaza at 9 p.m. with a running engine.

If the seller will not show ID or the permit, you do not have a deal. You have a story. Walk.

What do CARFAX Canada and lien checks catch and miss?

A history report shows recorded accidents, odometer readings, branding, service visits and U.S. history, but not cash repairs, unclaimed floods or mechanical condition. A lien search is a separate check, so buy a report that includes one or use Ontario’s UVIP.

A vehicle history report can show accidents reported to participating insurers and shops, odometer readings that were recorded, salvage/rebuild branding in some databases, service visits, and U.S. history if the VIN crossed the border. It does not show every fender-bender paid in cash, every flood that was not claimed, or whether the timing chain is quiet.

Liens are a separate legal fact: someone else may still have a security interest. CARFAX Canada’s order page (checked 15 Oct 2026) lists the Vehicle History Report at $58.95 and the Vehicle History Report + Lien Check at $77.95. Its lien search covers each province or territory where the VIN is or was registered, excluding the Northwest Territories, and its help centre says a lien check cannot be added to a report later. Ontario’s UVIP also reports lien information from PPSA / Repair and Storage Liens searches. Use both when you can. A “clean CARFAX” without a lien search is a marketing phrase.

Recalls: search Transport Canada’s Motor Vehicle Safety Recalls Database by make, model and year, then use the manufacturer’s VIN recall lookup (Transport Canada lists the links) to see whether that exact car is affected. A history report that mentions recalls is a hint, not the official list.

What is Ontario’s UVIP, and what do other provinces use?

The UVIP is Ontario’s $20 used vehicle information package, and the private seller must give it to the buyer under the Highway Traffic Act. Other provinces use their own records, so read that government’s page for the province where the car will be plated.

Ontario.ca (updated 1 Oct 2026): the package includes the vehicle details, its Ontario registration history with odometer readings, lien information, the average wholesale value (the minimum value Ontario’s retail sales tax applies to), the last known status (for example, registered as unfit or reported as wrecked), and a bill of sale section.

Fee: $20 (O. Reg. 601/93, s. 5). Order online with the VIN or plate (mailed within 5 business days, with up to 10 days for delivery), or buy it the same day at a ServiceOntario centre. The buyer can also buy one — useful if the seller “forgot.” OMVIC-registered dealers do not need to provide a UVIP.

Other provinces do not copy UVIP. They have their own used-vehicle information, ICBC history, SAAQ file, or buyer-beware plus a lien search you run yourself. If you are plating in B.C., Alberta, or Québec, read that government’s “buy a used vehicle” page the morning of the deal. Do not file an Ontario UVIP in Winnipeg and call it done.

Why is an independent pre-purchase inspection (PPI) non-negotiable?

Paperwork checks do not look underneath the car; an inspection you book, before money moves, does. CAA-Quebec puts the average cost at $150 to $250 and calls a seller’s refusal a major red flag.

Pick a shop that does not sell the car. Book a PPI after the documents look clean and before money moves. Ask for compression or scan, underbody, rust (salt provinces), accident repairs, tire date codes, brake measurements, and “would you let a family member buy this.”

Seller objections:

  • “My mechanic already looked” — that is not independent.
  • “I don’t have time” — then you don’t have a buyer.
  • “It’s sold as-is” — as-is is why you inspect, not why you skip.

A failed PPI is a price cut or a walk; CAA-Quebec notes the report is a bargaining tool for urgent repairs. Budget for it the way you budget for tax: CAA South Central Ontario lists its 139-point Vehicle Inspection Service at $179.95 for members and $199.95 for non-members (checked 15 Oct 2026). EVs need a shop that can read high-voltage health, not only oil.

What is the bill of sale, safety, tax and registration sequence?

In Ontario, the buyer registers at ServiceOntario within 6 days of the purchase and pays 13% retail sales tax there (in most cases), on the purchase price or the vehicle’s wholesale value, whichever is greater. You do not pay the tax to the seller.

Bring (per Ontario.ca, Buy or sell a used vehicle): proof of insurance, your Ontario driver’s licence, the UVIP, the bill of sale (signed and dated by both buyer and seller; the UVIP has a section for it), the Safety Standards Certificate if applicable, the permit with the Application for Transfer completed, and the odometer reading. The seller keeps the plates. A private seller advertising “no safety” is telling you the next bill. Wholesale value comes from the Canadian Red Book; if you paid less, you can get an appraisal before the transfer. Gifts between close family members can be tax-exempt with a sworn statement. Confirm the current ServiceOntario rules; this article will not compute your RST.

Other provinces: ICBC transfer appointments, SAAQ, MPI — each has a sequence. Insurance in force before you drive it home is the universal line. Quote the VIN first so you are not surprised at the kiosk.

  1. Documents + ID + VIN match.
  2. History + lien (UVIP and/or CARFAX Canada lien product + provincial search).
  3. PPI at your shop.
  4. Written bill of sale: names, addresses, date, VIN, odometer, price, “as-is” if true, lien status.
  5. Payment, then immediately transfer/register per provincial rules, with insurance binder in hand.

How should you pay a private seller safely?

Pay after the paperwork and the inspection, with a bank draft made out to the name on the permit or an in-branch transfer.

Bank draft issued to the name on the permit, or an in-branch transfer where both people sit with a teller, are the boring methods that work. Some buyers and sellers meet at the seller’s bank so the draft can be verified.

An e-transfer to a stranger for the full price is how people get emptied twice. Cash for a car is a robbery plot. Gift cards and crypto are scams.

Never send a deposit to “hold” a car you have not seen. Never pay extra to a shipping agent who emailed from a generic Gmail. If the price is thousands below book and they need you to move fast, it is not your lucky day.

What are the walk-away red flags?

Walk if the VIN or the seller’s name does not match the paperwork, a lien is still open, or the seller blocks an inspection.

  • VIN mismatch, painted-over VIN, or “I lost the permit.”
  • Seller not on title; pressure to use a different name on the bill of sale.
  • Open lien they will “pay off next week.” Discharge first, or pay the lienholder directly with documented payout.
  • Salvage/rebuild branding you did not price as a rebuild. Insurance may refuse or rate it as a different animal.
  • Odometer that jumped; cluster that does not match wear.
  • No PPI, no UVIP (Ontario), no daylight meeting.
  • Test drive only with a “mechanic friend” in the back seat who is clearly the real seller.

A used car can still be the cheaper TCO. The savings disappear if you inherit someone else’s loan or a rusted strut tower. Spend a few hundred dollars on paper and a lift first. Then decide.

Frequently asked questions

What is an Ontario UVIP and who pays for it?

A Used Vehicle Information Package is Ontario’s official snapshot of registration history, branding, odometer records, and lien information. The private seller is legally required to provide it (Highway Traffic Act). The fee is $20 (O. Reg. 601/93). Buyers can also purchase one at ServiceOntario. OMVIC-registered dealers do not have to provide one.

Is CARFAX Canada the same as a UVIP?

No. UVIP is Ontario government registration and PPSA/lien information. CARFAX Canada is a commercial history report (accidents, service, U.S. history). A history report without a lien search is incomplete. CARFAX Canada’s public prices, checked 15 Oct 2026, were $58.95 for the history report and $77.95 with a lien check — confirm carfax.ca.

Can I skip a pre-purchase inspection?

You can. You should not. A UVIP and a CARFAX do not look at the underbody. CAA-Quebec puts the average pre-purchase inspection at $150 to $250 and calls a seller’s refusal a major red flag. Walk if the seller blocks a shop you choose.

How should I pay a private seller?

Bank draft or in-branch transfer at a meeting both parties can live with, after paperwork and PPI. Avoid gift cards, crypto, cash in a parking lot at night, and e-transfers to a name that does not match the ownership. This is not legal advice.

How long do I have to register a used car in Ontario, and what tax do I pay?

You must register as the new owner at ServiceOntario within 6 days of the purchase. In most cases you pay 13% retail sales tax on the purchase price or the vehicle’s wholesale value, whichever is greater.

Does EVAP apply to a used EV?

No. Transport Canada says only new vehicles, registered for the first time, are eligible for the federal EVAP incentive. Some provinces offer used-EV incentives; check your province’s program page.
