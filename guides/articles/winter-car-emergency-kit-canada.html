Winter Driving Tips in Canada: Build Your Emergency Car Kit Without Overpacking

Start with what the government lists, use what you own, and buy the rest once · About 10 minutes · By Andrew · Updated 29 Oct 2026

This post contains Amazon.ca affiliate links. If you buy through them, Saving Optimizer may earn a commission at no extra cost to you.

The winter driving tips that matter most in Canada start before you leave: keep the tank at least half full, carry an emergency car kit, check the road report, and slow down on snow and ice. A good kit fits in one bin or duffel bag and covers four things: staying warm, being seen, getting unstuck, and calling for help. Start with the Government of Canada's emergency car kit list, which has 17 items (11 for the car and 6 for the trunk): food that won't spoil, water, a blanket, extra clothing and footwear, a first aid kit, a small shovel, a scraper and snowbrush, a flashlight, a whistle and a candle in a deep can with matches. In the trunk, add sand or non-clumping cat litter, washer fluid, jumper cables and warning lights. Most households already own half of this. Buy the rest as separate, decent-quality items rather than a cheap all-in-one kit full of things you will never use.

Key takeaways

  • Use the Government of Canada car kit list (17 items) as your checklist.
  • Pack for four jobs: stay warm, be seen, get unstuck, call for help.
  • Use what you already own first: blankets, old boots, a spare phone cable.
  • Buy jumper cables, a real shovel and a good scraper as separate items.

What should be in a winter car emergency kit in Canada?

The Government of Canada lists 11 items to keep in the car and 6 to keep in the trunk; Ontario adds a charged phone and traction mats. The federal page lists the basics to keep in the vehicle and in the trunk. Ontario's winter driving page adds a few practical items, such as a charged phone and traction mats. What do you probably already own?

Probably the whole warmth and food part of the list. Before buying anything, walk through the house. An old wool blanket or sleeping bag, last year's boots, a toque and mitts, a spare phone charging cable, a flashlight and a few granola bars cover the warmth and food part of the list for nothing. Water bottles from the pantry work if you replace them every six months, as the federal list suggests; plastic bottles are recommended because glass can break when frozen. A bag of non-clumping cat litter from the cupboard doubles as traction. The money you save here can go to the items where quality matters.

Which kit items are worth paying for?

Jumper cables, a real shovel and a long-handled snowbrush, plus a first aid kit with a seatbelt cutter. These are the items where cheap versions fail when you need them.

  • Jumper cables. Thin, short cables can struggle in deep cold. Choose cables long enough to reach between two cars parked nose to nose or side by side; a good option is a set of Energizer heavy-duty jumper cables with a carry bag. A portable jump starter like the NOCO Boost GB40 is an alternative, but it must be kept charged, so add recharging it to your yearly routine.
  • Shovel. A compact metal or sturdy plastic shovel that fits in the trunk, such as the Rhino USA folding shovel. The tiny shovels in some pre-made kits are hard to use in packed snow.
  • Scraper and snowbrush. A long-handled brush clears the roof, which Ontario's winter driving page tells drivers to do along with windows, lights and mirrors. A 2-in-1 tool such as the AstroAI 27-inch snow brush and ice scraper covers both jobs.
  • Flashlight. The federal list says wind-up, which avoids dead batteries. If you use a battery light, store the batteries separately or replace them each fall.
  • First aid kit. Look for one with a seatbelt cutter, as the federal list suggests, and add any personal medications.

Is a cheap pre-made kit worth it?

Usually only as a starting point. All-in-one kits can be convenient, but many pad the item count with things that look good on the box: dozens of bandages, a tiny tool set, a flimsy tow strap, or cables too short for real use. If you buy one, compare the contents with the official list and plan to replace the weak items. Often it costs less to buy three or four good items and fill in the rest from home. Avoid anything that needs a power source you do not have, and anything you would not know how to use at the side of a dark highway.

What should you do before you drive?

Fuel up, top up, check the road report and tell someone your plan. These habits matter more than anything in the bin.

  • Keep the tank at least half full. Ontario advises this to reduce moisture in the fuel system and add weight, and it also gives you heat if you are stuck.
  • Use winter washer fluid. Ontario recommends washer fluid that works down to -40°C.
  • Check the road report. Ontario 511 shows road conditions and where plows are on provincial highways. Most provinces have a 511 service.
  • Delay the trip if the forecast looks bad, as Ontario suggests, and tell someone where you are going, your route and when you expect to arrive, as Transport Canada advises.
  • Clear the whole car. Ontario tells drivers to clear ice and snow from windows, lights, mirrors and the roof.
  • Keep the phone charged and a cable in the car.

What are the most important winter driving tips on the road?

Slow down, leave more space, and watch for black ice. Ontario's winter driving page lists these on-road tips:

  • Slow down. Many winter collisions happen because drivers are going too fast for the road conditions.
  • Skip cruise control on wet, snowy or icy pavement; it reduces your reaction time and control.
  • Steer, brake and accelerate gently. If you skid, release the brakes and steer in the direction you want to go.
  • Leave extra space. It takes longer to stop on slippery roads.
  • Read the road surface. Winter asphalt should look grey-white; if it looks black and shiny, it could be ice. Shaded areas, bridges and overpasses freeze earlier.
  • Never pass a working snowplow. Roads are plowed in sections, so you usually won't be behind a plow for more than 20 to 30 minutes.

Are winter tires required where you live?

In Quebec and on many B.C. highways, yes; in Ontario, no.

Ontario's winter driving page says winter tires can shorten braking distances by as much as 25%, and that all-season tires can start to lose grip at 7°C. In Quebec, winter tires are mandatory from 1 December to 15 March for vehicles registered in the province, with a fine of $200 to $300. In British Columbia, winter tires or chains are required on most designated routes from 1 October to 30 April; on select highways that are not through mountain passes or high-snowfall areas, the requirement ends 31 March. B.C. lists a $121 fine for a passenger vehicle without winter tires where they are required. In Ontario, winter tires are not mandatory, but many insurers offer a discount; see the Ontario winter tire insurance discount and winter tires on a budget.

What should you do if you get stuck in the snow?

Stay in the car if it is not at risk of being hit, keep the tailpipe clear, and call for help. Transport Canada's winter driving page says:

  1. Stay inside the vehicle for shelter if it is not at risk of being hit by other drivers. Going out into a storm puts you at risk of getting lost or suffering in the cold.
  2. Call for help, and set out a warning light, reflective safety triangles or flares. Turn on the dome light; overusing headlights can run the battery down.
  3. Make sure the tailpipe is not blocked by snow, keep a window open a bit on the side protected from the wind, and run the motor as little as possible to reduce the risk of carbon monoxide.
  4. Put on the extra clothing and use the blanket before you get cold; a hat helps.
  5. Don't do heavy lifting, shovelling or pushing in bitter cold. Use the shovel and traction material only once conditions allow and it is safe.

Do you already have roadside assistance?

You might. Before paying for a separate roadside plan, check whether you already have one. Some car insurance policies offer it as an add-on, some new-vehicle warranties include it for a few years, and some credit cards include basic roadside help. Read what each covers, such as towing distance and battery boosts, so you do not pay twice. Our guide to winter car maintenance costs covers the rest of the season.

When should you refresh the kit?

Once a year, in the fall. Each fall, when the tires change, open the kit. Replace the water and food, test the flashlight, check the first aid kit, put the warm clothes back, and top up washer fluid. In spring, take out what you do not need for summer so it does not fill the trunk all year.

Frequently asked questions

What are the best winter driving tips in Canada?

Before you leave, keep the tank at least half full, carry an emergency car kit, check the 511 road report and tell someone your route. On the road, slow down, skip cruise control on snow or ice, leave extra space, watch for black ice on bridges and overpasses, and never pass a working snowplow.

What should be in a winter car emergency kit in Canada?

The Government of Canada lists food that won't spoil, water, a blanket, extra clothing and shoes, a first aid kit, a small shovel, scraper and snowbrush, a candle in a deep can with matches, a wind-up flashlight, a whistle and roadmaps, plus sand or cat litter, washer fluid, a tow rope, jumper cables, a fire extinguisher and warning lights in the trunk.

Is a pre-made car emergency kit worth it?

It can be convenient, but many include low-quality cables and tiny shovels. Compare the contents with the official list and replace weak items, or build your own from home and a few purchases.

Why keep the gas tank half full in winter?

Ontario advises it to reduce moisture in the fuel system and add weight to the vehicle. It also means you have fuel for heat if you are stuck.

Are winter tires mandatory in Canada?

Not everywhere. Quebec requires them from 1 December to 15 March. B.C. requires winter tires or chains on most designated routes from 1 October to 30 April, ending 31 March on select highways. Ontario does not require them.

How often should I replace water in my car kit?

The Government of Canada suggests changing the water every six months and using plastic bottles so they won't break if frozen.

What should I do if I am stuck in the snow?

Stay inside the vehicle if it is not at risk of being hit, call for help, keep the tailpipe clear of snow, keep a window open a bit on the side away from the wind, run the motor as little as possible, and avoid heavy shovelling or pushing in bitter cold.
