Natural gas vs electric heat cost in Canada: how to compare for your home

Useful heat, not sticker ¢/m³ vs ¢/kWh · About 15 minutes · 20 Sep 2026

Households compare a gas “¢/m³” screenshot to an electricity “¢/kWh” screenshot and pick a fuel. Those units are not the same thing, and neither line is the bill. Delivery, carbon, equipment efficiency, and whether the heat is burned or moved decide January.

This is a Canadian conversion worksheet: GJ vs kWh, furnace AFUE vs heat-pump COP, and provincial adders at a high level. It is not a U.S. Henry Hub chart. Renters comparing listings should use heat included vs hydro first. Owners picking equipment should continue to heat pump vs furnace.

Key takeaways

  • Convert to useful heat before you compare. 1 m³ of natural gas ≈ 0.0373 GJ ≈ 10.36 kWh of energy; a 95% furnace delivers about 9.8 kWh of heat. Electric resistance is 1:1. A heat pump divides your ¢/kWh by its COP.
  • OEB Enbridge supply (1 Jul 2026): EGD zone 9.0498 ¢/m³; Union South 17.3871 ¢/m³. That is not the winter bill — delivery, carbon, and the customer charge dwarf a cheap commodity month.
  • Provincial patterns matter: Ontario and Prairie gas vs Ontario hydro; Hydro-Québec Rate D can flip electric heat; Alberta shops the energy line and still pays delivery.
  • Heat pumps change the electric side of the worksheet and do not erase panel, duct, or −25°C performance questions — see the companion comparison.
  • Canada Greener Homes Grant and Loan are closed to new applicants; OHPA intake closed 31 Jul 2026. Price the retrofit in cash and live provincial / utility stacks, not a 2023 grant blog.

Convert apples to apples: GJ vs kWh and equipment efficiency

Natural gas is billed in cubic metres (or GJ in some provinces). Electricity is billed in kWh. The physical bridge:

  • 1 m³ of natural gas ≈ 0.0373 GJ ≈ 10.36 kWh of energy content (standard conversion; your utility’s heat value is on the bill or tariff).
  • 1 GJ = 277.8 kWh.
  • Furnace AFUE is how much of that energy becomes house heat. A 95% condensing furnace: 10.36 × 0.95 ≈ 9.8 kWh useful per m³. An 80% mid-efficiency unit: ≈ 8.3 kWh.
  • Electric resistance (baseboard, furnace coil, plenum heater): COP ≈ 1. One kWh in, one kWh of heat.
  • Heat pump: COP is heat moved per kWh. At COP 2.5 you buy 0.40 kWh of electricity per useful kWh. At −20°C that COP may be 1.5 or the unit may hand off to backup.

The comparison number you want is cents per useful kWh:

  • Gas: (all-in ¢ per m³) ÷ useful kWh per m³.
  • Resistance electric: your all-in ¢/kWh (commodity + delivery + regulators − any rebate, from a real bill).
  • Heat pump: all-in ¢/kWh ÷ seasonal COP you actually believe, not the brochure COP at +8°C.

Heat pumps change the electric side of the comparison

A heat pump is not “electric heat” in the baseboard sense. It moves outdoor heat indoors. That is why a Québec or Manitoba kWh can beat gas even when an Ontario baseboard cannot. It is also why a cheap mini-split that locks out at −15°C and throws a resistance coil is just a fancy baseboard on the worst days.

Walk through COP, backup, and hybrid changeover in heat pump vs furnace. Put those COP assumptions back into this worksheet. Do not paste a manufacturer’s “up to 18 HSPF” into a Winnipeg January.

Existing ductwork, panel capacity, and cold-climate performance

The fuel comparison assumes you can actually run the equipment.

  • Ducts. A furnace already has them. A central heat pump wants enough airflow. Undersized 1970s ducts make noise and short-cycle. Ductless heads skip ducts and add wall holes and a snow line for the outdoor unit.
  • Panel. Resistance backup and some cold-climate compressors want 200 A and spare breakers. A 100 A service can make the “cheap electric” option a $3,000–$8,000 panel project before the first useful kWh.
  • Cold climate. Ask for capacity and COP at −15°C and −25°C, not “operates to.” Hybrid dual-fuel is how many Ontario and Prairie houses keep gas for the deep freeze.

If the house is a sieve, neither fuel wins. Seal and insulate first — attic R-values and EnerGuide are the decision tools, not a new burner.

Operating cost vs retrofit capital — simple payback framing

Write four numbers on one page:

  1. Annual useful-heat cost on the current system (12 months of bills, converted).
  2. Modelled annual cost on the alternative (same useful heat, new ¢/useful kWh).
  3. Net capital after live rebates only. Federal Greener Homes Grant: closed; last document day for existing files was 31 Dec 2025. Loan: applications closed, funding committed (NRCan page dated 17 Apr 2026). OHPA: last application day 31 Jul 2026; existing applicants have document deadlines into 2027. Provincial and utility stacks still move — verify the week you sign.
  4. Simple payback = net capital ÷ annual operating delta. If you will sell in three years, a 12-year payback is a gift to the next owner.

A $12,000 heat-pump increment that saves $600/year is 20 years before comfort, cooling, and carbon — which you may still want, but that is not a “gas is expensive” slogan.

When staying on gas still wins for a near-term budget

  • The furnace is mid-life, the heat exchanger is sound, and this winter’s emergency is a $400 repair, not a new plant.
  • The panel cannot take a compressor plus backup without a service upgrade you cannot fund this year.
  • You are on cheap gas commodity and modest delivery, in a tight house, in a cold climate where a heat pump would run backup all January.
  • You are a tenant. You do not buy the furnace. You buy the lease — see Housing.

Staying on gas is not a moral failure. It is a cash-flow choice. Revisit when the furnace dies, when a live provincial stack appears, or when an EnerGuide report shows the envelope is ready. For the Ontario clock on the electric side, use TOU vs ULO vs Tiered. For Québec’s Rate D math, use Hydro-Québec Rate D.

Frequently asked questions

Can I compare ¢/m³ of gas to ¢/kWh of electricity directly?

No. Convert gas to useful kWh using energy content (~10.36 kWh/m³) and furnace AFUE, then compare to electricity ¢/kWh (or ¢/kWh ÷ COP for a heat pump). Add delivery and carbon from the bill, not from the commodity screenshot.

Why is my Enbridge line higher than the OEB 9 ¢ supply figure?

The 9.0498 ¢/m³ EGD figure (1 Jul 2026) is gas supply in one rate zone. Union South supply is 17.3871 ¢/m³. Delivery, transportation, carbon, and the monthly customer charge are extra and often larger than supply in a mild month.

Does a heat pump always beat gas in Canada?

No. It depends on your all-in ¢/kWh, the pump’s cold-weather COP, backup fuel, and capital. Québec’s Rate D often favours electric heat pumps; an Ontario leaky house on resistance backup often does not.

Are Greener Homes or OHPA still open?

New Grant applications are closed (existing-file documents were due 31 Dec 2025). The Loan is closed and funding is committed. OHPA intake closed 31 Jul 2026; remaining applicants have later document deadlines. Check provincial and utility programs instead.

Should a renter switch heating fuel?

Usually no. Compare heat-included vs hydro on the lease. Equipment choices belong to the owner. Air sealing you can undo is still fair game.
