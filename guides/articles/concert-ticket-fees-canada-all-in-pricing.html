Concert Ticket Fees in Canada: What Drip Pricing Law Means and How to Compare the Real Price

Compare the total on the screen where you pay · About 10 minutes · By Andrew · Updated 1 Oct 2026

Ticket fees in Canada are legal, but hiding them is not. Under the Competition Act's drip pricing rule, a seller cannot advertise a price that cannot be obtained because mandatory fixed fees, other than taxes, are added later. In January 2026 the Federal Court of Appeal upheld a $38.978 million penalty against Cineplex over a $1.50 online booking fee that was drip pricing. In September 2026 the Federal Court ruled that itemized ticket fees are not drip pricing when every mandatory charge appears together on the same screen, and that federal law does not require a single all-in price. Ontario and Quebec ticket rules go further and require the total price. In practice, compare tickets by the final total before you pay, not by the headline price.

Key takeaways

  • Drip pricing, advertising a price that cannot be obtained because mandatory fees are added later, is prohibited by the Competition Act. Taxes are the exception.
  • 21 Jan 2026: the Federal Court of Appeal upheld the $38.978 million penalty against Cineplex over its $1.50 online booking fee.
  • 22 Sep 2026: in Kuling v Ticketmaster, the Federal Court held that itemized fees shown together on the same screen are not drip pricing, and that there is no federal all-in pricing requirement.
  • Ontario's Ticket Sales Act requires the total price in Canadian dollars with fees itemized, and the total printed on the ticket. In Quebec, the OPC says an advertised price must be all-inclusive; sales taxes may be left out.
  • Compare the final total, per ticket, at the last screen before payment.
  • Example with made-up inputs: a $79.50 ticket comes to $112.15 with fees and tax, while an $89 all-in ticket comes to $100.57.

What drip pricing means

In 2022 Parliament added an express drip pricing provision to the Competition Act. It treats as false or misleading a price representation that is not attainable because fixed obligatory charges or fees must be paid on top of it, unless those are government charges such as sales tax. The Competition Bureau's guidance is that the advertised price should include all mandatory fees, with only taxes added.

The rule targets the pattern most ticket buyers know: a headline price on the event page, then service fees, facility charges and order fees appearing at checkout. If the fees are mandatory and were not in the price you were shown, the price you were shown was not the real price.

The Cineplex case

The Competition Tribunal found in 2024 that Cineplex's online booking fee, $1.50 a ticket for most customers, was drip pricing, because the advertised ticket price could not be obtained online without it. It ordered an administrative monetary penalty of $38.978 million. On 21 January 2026 the Federal Court of Appeal dismissed Cineplex's appeal and upheld the penalty. Cineplex said it would seek leave to appeal to the Supreme Court of Canada.

The September 2026 Ticketmaster ruling

In Kuling v Ticketmaster, decided on 22 September 2026, the Federal Court declined to certify a proposed class action over a $2.50 order processing fee. The Court held that itemizing fees is not drip pricing when every mandatory charge is clearly visible on the same screen at the same time; drip pricing requires both concealment and a sequence in which fees are added later. The Court also said federal law does not require a single all-in price; that is for Parliament to decide. Commentators noted that it is a single Federal Court decision and that a different page design could be treated differently.

Provincial rules: Ontario and Quebec

Provincial law can require more than the Competition Act. Ontario's Ticket Sales Act, 2017 requires primary sellers to disclose the total price of a ticket in Canadian dollars, including an itemized list of fees, service charges and taxes, and to print or display the total price paid on the ticket. For resale, Ontario caps the resale price at the original total price, plus taxes and the platform's standard fees, and requires a written money-back guarantee or proof of validity. In Quebec, the Office de la protection du consommateur says that when a merchant advertises a price it must be an all-inclusive price covering everything you will pay, with the total shown more prominently than its components; certain amounts, such as sales taxes, may be excluded. If you are charged more than the advertised price, you can insist on paying the advertised price. Other provinces rely mainly on general consumer protection law.

How to compare the real price

  1. Go to the last screen before payment for each option, with the same number of tickets.
  2. Write down the total, including every fee and tax.
  3. Divide by the number of tickets for a per-ticket total.
  4. Note any per-order fee. It matters more for one ticket than for four.
  5. Compare delivery options. Mobile tickets are usually free; some sellers charge for other methods.
  6. Take a screenshot of the total before paying.

Ways to pay fewer fees

  • Buy at the venue box office in person where it sells without online fees.
  • Buy several tickets in one order if a per-order fee applies.
  • Use the venue's or team's own season or membership pre-sales, which sometimes have lower fees.
  • For smaller venues, buy from the venue or the artist's site.
  • Choose mobile delivery over paid delivery options.
  • Do not pay for optional add-ons you will not use, such as ticket insurance you do not need.

When to complain, and to whom

If an advertised price could not be obtained because of mandatory fees added later, you can report it to the Competition Bureau. For a sale in Ontario, ticket rules are enforced by Consumer Protection Ontario; in Quebec, by the Office de la protection du consommateur. For a ticket that turns out to be fake, report it to the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre and your card issuer. Keep screenshots of the price at each step.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing headline prices instead of checkout totals.
  • Paying a per-order fee three times by buying tickets in separate orders.
  • Buying from a look-alike resale site because it appeared first in search results.
  • Not taking a screenshot of the total before paying.
  • Assuming a resale price above face value is allowed in Ontario; it is capped.

Frequently asked questions

Are ticket fees legal in Canada?

Yes, but they cannot be hidden. The Competition Act prohibits drip pricing, which is advertising a price that cannot be obtained because mandatory fixed fees, other than taxes, are added later. Ontario and Quebec also require the total price to be shown.

What happened in the Cineplex drip pricing case?

The Competition Tribunal found Cineplex's $1.50 online booking fee was drip pricing and ordered a $38.978 million penalty. On 21 January 2026 the Federal Court of Appeal dismissed Cineplex's appeal and upheld the penalty. Cineplex said it would seek leave to appeal to the Supreme Court of Canada.

Does Ticketmaster have to show an all-in price?

In Kuling v Ticketmaster, decided 22 September 2026, the Federal Court held that federal law does not require a single all-in price and that itemized fees shown together on the same screen are not drip pricing. Ontario's Ticket Sales Act and Quebec's rules still require the total price.
