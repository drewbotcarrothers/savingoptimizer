How to Shop Auto Insurance in Canada: Broker vs Direct vs Aggregator Without Getting Lost

Compare coverages, not headlines · About 16 minutes · 16 Sep 2026

Canadians renew auto insurance on autopilot, then complain about the number. Ratehub’s 2026 ownership build uses about $164/month ($1,973/year) as a national insurance line after a 14.4% Applied index bump — a sketch, not your premium. Ontario, B.C., and Québec do not even share a market structure. The job is to shop like-for-like once a year, not to chase the cheapest tile on a comparison site.

This is comparison education. Saving Optimizer is not an insurance broker, agent, or insurer and does not sell or bind policies. For Ontario consumer rules we lean on FSRA’s public pages. Pair this with car TCO so insurance sits next to parking and fuel, and with the Insurance category when those guides land.

That would not make us your broker. Only a licensed broker, agent, or company can sell you a policy in your province. We do not claim to find you the cheapest rate.

Key takeaways

  • Get at least three quotes on identical coverage — FSRA’s own shopping advice.
  • Brokers, direct writers, and aggregators are different jobs. Use more than one channel.
  • Do not switch mid-term to a teaser without pricing cancellation penalties.
  • Ask, every year, about winter tires (mandatory discount in Ontario), multi-policy, mileage, and telematics.
  • A too-good premium with a coverage gap is not a save. It is an uninsured loss waiting for a winter night.

What brokers, direct insurers, and aggregators each do

Brokers sell for multiple insurers they represent. FSRA notes you may ask an Ontario broker for the names of those companies. They can help with bundling and odd risks. They cannot invent a market they do not hold.

Direct writers sell their own brand, often online. Sometimes the web price is unavailable through a broker. Sometimes it is worse. You only know if you quote both.

Aggregators (comparison / quote tools) show a panel. The panel is not the whole industry. Insurers that skip lead-gen sites will not appear. Use the output as a list of names to verify, not as a binding offer.

A clean shopping round: one broker (ask which markets), one or two directs you might actually buy, and optionally one aggregator to catch a name you missed. Three real quotes. Not twelve half-filled forms.

Compare identical coverage and deductibles—not headline premiums

Insurers do not charge the same price for the same benefits — FSRA says so explicitly. They also do not quote the same benefits unless you force them to.

  • Third-party liability: Ontario’s legal minimum is $200,000; many households carry $1 million or $2 million. Match the limit.
  • Collision and comprehensive deductibles: $500 vs $1,000 changes the premium and the overnight bill.
  • Accident benefits: Ontario has optional increases and reductions. A cheap quote may have dropped coverage you still want. This is regulated nuance — confirm with a licensed person, not a forum screenshot.
  • Listed drivers, annual km, commute vs pleasure, postal code, VIN.

If a quote is $400 lower and you cannot see those fields, it is not cheaper. It is incomplete.

Provincial quirks: Ontario FSRA tips vs other provinces

Ontario: private market. FSRA consumer pages cover how to buy, how to save, and what is in a standard policy. Winter-tire discounts are required in rating. RIBO for brokers.

British Columbia: ICBC provides basic coverage; optional collision/comprehensive is a shopping problem with ICBC and private optional insurers. Do not paste an Ontario broker workflow onto a B.C. basic policy.

Québec: SAAQ covers bodily injury on the public side; private insurers cover property damage and other optional coverages. You are shopping a different product than an Ontario package policy.

Prairies and Atlantic: mix of private markets and public crowns (Saskatchewan, Manitoba). The shopping channel still exists for optional add-ons even where basic is public. Ask the provincial regulator’s consumer page which layer you are quoting.

Discounts checklist: multi-policy, winter tires, telematics, mileage

FSRA’s discount list (reviewed Sep 2026) is a script you can read off the phone:

  • Multi-policy (home/tenant + auto): often described as 5–15%.
  • Multi-vehicle: 5–15% range in the same FSRA note.
  • Winter tires: required offering in Ontario; ask the percent, the season dates, and whether all-weather tires qualify before you buy a set.
  • Low mileage / pleasure use: tell them if you no longer commute.
  • Telematics / usage-based: can be a large percentage if you drive calmly; it can also raise the renewal if you do not. Get the non-telematics premium too.
  • Group, retiree, mature driver, defensive driving, renewal loyalty, alarms.

You must actually qualify. A winter-tire discount without four qualifying tires installed is a future claim argument, not a save.

How often to re-shop (and why mid-term switches can hurt)

FSRA: the company that won last year may not win this year, and you should not switch midway through the year to avoid cancellation penalties. Put a reminder 45 days before renewal. Shop then. Bind when the current term ends, unless staying is clearly cheaper.

Mid-term reasons that can still make sense: you sold the car, you moved provinces, a claim made the relationship unworkable. Those are life events, not a $12/month teaser.

Red flags: too-good rates with coverage gaps

  • Pressure to bind before you read the wordings.
  • Unlicensed Facebook “brokers.” Check RIBO / FSRA / provincial registers.
  • Collision dropped on a financed car (your lender will not love that, and neither will you in a ditch).
  • Named-driver exclusions you did not notice.
  • A telematics discount that requires a dongle you will unplug.

Insurance is a large slice of used vs new TCO and of whether a second car should exist. Shop it like a system, once a year, with a worksheet — not like a flash sale.

Frequently asked questions

Is a broker always cheaper than buying direct?

No. Brokers can quote several insurers they represent; direct writers quote only their own product, which may include online-only discounts a broker cannot access. FSRA tells Ontario consumers to get at least three quotes and to compare coverages, not just premiums. Saving Optimizer is not a broker and does not place policies.

Should I switch auto insurance mid-term to a lower quote?

Usually wait until renewal. FSRA’s consumer page warns that switching midway through the year can trigger cancellation penalties that erase the savings. Ask both the current insurer and the new one what a mid-term move would cost in writing.

Do Ontario insurers have to offer a winter-tire discount?

Yes. Ontario requires insurers to include a winter-tire discount in their rating for private-passenger policies. The percentage (often described in consumer explainers as about 2–5%), qualifying dates, and whether all-weather tires count are insurer-specific. Ask before you buy tires, then send the invoice.

What should I match when I compare quotes?

Third-party liability limit, collision and comprehensive deductibles, accident-benefits options, and listed drivers. A $200 cheaper premium with a $2,000 deductible and stripped accident benefits is not a win. Use a worksheet so every quote uses the same facts.

Are quote websites the same as brokers?

No. Aggregators and comparison sites collect leads and show some of the market. They are not licensed to bind every insurer, and they are not a substitute for a RIBO-licensed broker in Ontario (or the equivalent regulator where you live). Treat them as a starting list, then verify the policy wording.
