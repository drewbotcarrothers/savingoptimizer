How to cut home energy costs in a Canadian winter

Heating, habits, and upgrades that pay back · About 11 minutes · Reviewed 2026

In a Canadian house or condo, the energy bill is mostly a heating story. Air conditioning matters in a southern Ontario August or a Lower Mainland heat wave, but January is where the money goes — natural gas, electricity, oil, propane, or a mix.

Gadgets are not the first move. The first move is stopping heated air from leaving, then using less of it, then paying a fair rate for what remains. Big equipment comes last, when you know the house.

How do you find out what you actually pay for energy?

Pull the last twelve months of bills from your utility or retailer. You want two numbers: how much energy you used (kWh, m³ of gas, litres of oil) and what you paid including delivery charges. Delivery can be a large slice, especially on electricity in some provinces. You cannot shop delivery away. You can shop usage down.

If you live in a condo, ask what the corporation covers. A low hydro bill can hide heating in the maintenance fee. You still benefit from less waste; you just may not see it as a line item.

Why seal air leaks before buying gadgets?

Weatherstripping on exterior doors, a sweep on the bottom of the door, caulking around window trim, and foam gaskets behind outlet covers on exterior walls are unglamorous and cheap. They often return more per dollar than a smart gadget.

On a windy day, walk the house. If you can feel air at the attic hatch, the rim joist in an unfinished basement, or the exhaust flap of an old dryer, you have found winter dollars. Close the chimney damper if you have a wood fireplace you are not using. A glass door on that fireplace helps even when the damper is tired.

Safety first: do not block combustion-air inlets or tamper with a furnace, water heater, or gas fireplace. If a room smells like gas or a carbon monoxide alarm chirps, that is not a savings project. That is a phone call.

What thermostat habits work in a Canadian house?

A common, comfortable target is about 20°C when people are home and awake, and 16–17°C overnight or at work, if the house can recover without blasting. Older boilers and some heat pumps dislike deep setbacks; if the house takes three hours to feel human, use a shallower drop.

Heat the rooms you live in. Close doors to unused guest rooms. Do not heat a garage you do not work in. Open south-facing blinds on sunny winter days; close them at dusk. That is free passive solar, and it works as far north as most of us live.

If you have a programmable or learning thermostat, program it. A “smart” thermostat that nobody set is a clock with opinions.

How do you choose the right electricity or gas rate plan?

Ontario still uses time-of-use and, in some homes, ultra-low overnight pricing. Shifting laundry, dishwasher, and EV charging into off-peak hours is one of the few “do this tonight” moves that shows up on a bill. Other provinces have flatter residential rates; the win there is usage, not the clock.

Alberta households can choose a retailer and a fixed or floating rate. Compare the total offer, not just the energy price, and read the term. In regulated provinces, you are mostly stuck with the utility — which is fine. Focus on the house.

Hydro-Québec and some hydro-rich utilities have historically lower electricity prices; cheap power is not a reason to heat the neighbourhood through a leaky patio door.

How do you cut hot water, laundry, and quiet loads?

Water heating is often the second-largest energy use. Shorten showers, fix the hot tap that sings, and wash clothes in cold water (modern detergents are built for it). A dryer is a furnace for your socks; a full load and clean lint trap help, and a drying rack in a spare room helps more.

LED bulbs are settled science. Replace the fixtures you use every night. Leave the hall closet for later. Unplug or switch off the always-on extras: the second fridge in the basement that holds six condiments, the old freezer in the garage that costs more than the burgers it protects. If you keep a chest freezer, fill empty space with jugs of water so it cycles less.

When do filters, furnaces, and heat pumps pay back?

A dirty furnace filter makes the fan work harder and the house less even. Check it every one to three months in heating season. Book the annual service. A $15 filter is cheaper than a January no-heat call-out.

Heat pumps — air-source, cold-climate models, or dual-fuel with a gas furnace — can cut energy use a lot in the right house. They are not a weekend project. Ask what the unit is rated to do at −15°C or −25°C, what backup heat you will have, and whether your ductwork or electrical panel can handle it. Provincial and utility rebates change; check your utility and provincial energy site before you sign, and get the rebate rules in writing.

Attic insulation is the other unsexy winner. If you have six inches of sad batting and a frosty attic hatch, more insulation often beats a conversation about windows. Windows are last unless they are failing or you are renovating the wall anyway.

What is a 30-day plan to cut the energy bill?

  1. Week 1: read twelve months of bills. Change filters. Drop the overnight setpoint by a degree or two.
  2. Week 2: door sweeps, outlet gaskets, attic hatch, and the dryer vent.
  3. Week 3: cold-water laundry, full dishwasher, off-peak if you have it. Retire the vanity fridge if you can.
  4. Week 4: list one capital item (insulation, thermostat, heat-pump quote) and get a real number — not a vibe.

Households that do the boring work first often see a noticeable winter bill without a renovation. The renovation can still be worth it. It just should not be the first chapter.
